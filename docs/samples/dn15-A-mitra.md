# 2.1 `Paṭiccasamuppāda` (dependent origination)

## Mahānidānasutta

## `Paṭiccasamuppāda` (dependent origination)

95. Thus have I heard: at one time the Bhagavā dwells among the Kurus, in a market town of the Kurus named Kammāsadhamma. Then the Venerable Ānanda approached the Bhagavā; having approached and paid homage to the Bhagavā, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the venerable Ānanda said this to the Bhagavā: “It is wonderful, Bhante; it is marvelous, Bhante. “Bhante, although this `paṭiccasamuppādo` (dependent origination) is so deep and appears so profound, yet to me it seems as plain as if it were quite obvious.” “Do not say so, Ānanda; do not say so, Ānanda. And this dependent origination, Ānanda, is deep and appears deep. Ānanda, through the non-comprehension and non-penetration of this Dhamma, this generation has become like a tangled skein, like a knotted ball of thread, like matted rushes and reeds; it does not transcend the plane of misery, the bad destination, the lower realms, and the cycle of saṃsāra.

96. “Ānanda, when asked: ‘Is there a specific condition for old age and death?’ one should say: ‘There is.’ If it should be asked: ‘With what as condition is there old age and death?’ one should say to him: ‘With birth as condition, there is old age and death.’”

“Ānanda, when asked, ‘Is there a specific condition for birth?’, one should say, ‘There is.’ If it should be asked, ‘What is the condition for birth?’, one should say to him, ‘Existence is the condition for birth.’”

“‘Is there, due to this condition, existence?’—being asked thus, Ānanda, one should say: ‘There is.’ If he should say, ‘Due to what condition is there existence?’, one should say to him: ‘Due to the condition of clinging, there is existence.’”

“Ānanda, if one is asked, ‘Is there a specific condition for clinging?’ one should answer, ‘There is.’ If it should be asked, ‘With what as condition does clinging arise?’ one should answer, ‘Clinging arises with craving as its condition.’”

“Ānanda, if one is asked, ‘Does craving exist due to a specific condition?’ one should say, ‘It does.’ If it should be asked, ‘Due to what condition does craving exist?’ one should say, ‘Craving exists due to the condition of feeling.’”

“Ānanda, if one is asked, ‘Is there a specific condition for feeling?’ one should answer, ‘There is.’ If it should be asked, ‘By what condition is there feeling?’ one should answer, ‘Feeling is conditioned by contact.’”

“Ānanda, if one is asked, ‘Does contact exist through this specific condition?’ one should say, ‘It exists.’ If it should be asked, ‘Through what condition does contact arise?’ one should say to him, ‘Contact arises with name-and-form as its condition.’”

“Ānanda, if one is asked, ‘Is there a specific condition for name-and-form?’ one should say, ‘There is.’ If it should be asked, ‘By what condition is there name-and-form?’ one should say, ‘By the condition of consciousness there is name-and-form.’”

“Ānanda, if one is asked, ‘Is consciousness conditioned by this?’ one should say, ‘It exists.’ If one should say, ‘Conditioned by what is consciousness?’ one should say to him, ‘Consciousness is conditioned by name-and-form.’”

97. Thus, Ānanda, with name-and-form as condition, `viññāṇaṃ` (consciousness) arises; with consciousness as condition, name-and-form arises; with name-and-form as condition, `phasso` (contact) arises; with contact as condition, `vedanā` (feeling) arises; with feeling as condition, `taṇhā` (craving) arises; with craving as condition, `upādānaṃ` (clinging) arises; with clinging as condition, `bhavo` (existence) arises; with existence as condition, `jāti` (birth) arises; and with birth as condition, `jarāmaraṇaṃ` (old age and death), sorrow, lamentation, pain, grief, and despair come to be. Such is the `samudayo` (origin) of this entire `dukkhakkhandha` (mass of suffering).

98. ‘With `jāti` (birth) as condition, there is aging and death’—this, indeed, has been said. In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, with birth as condition, there is aging and death. For if, Ānanda, birth were not to occur in any way, for anyone, anywhere—namely, of devas in their state as devas, of gandhabbas in their state as gandhabbas, of yakkhas in their state as yakkhas, of bhūtas in their state as bhūtas, of humans in their state as humans, of quadrupeds in their state as quadrupeds, of birds in their state as birds, or of reptiles in their state as reptiles—if, Ānanda, birth were not to occur for those various beings in their respective states. When there is no birth in every respect, with the cessation of birth, would aging and death be discerned? “No, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for `jarāmaraṇaṃ` (old age and death), namely: birth.

99. “It was said, ‘With `bhavo` (existence) as condition, `jāti` (birth) comes to be.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how with existence as condition, birth comes to be. For if, Ānanda, there were no existence at all—in any way, in any manner, for anyone, anywhere—namely, whether `kāmabhavo` (existence in the realm of sense desire), `rūpabhavo` (existence in the realm of form), or `arūpabhavo` (existence in the formless realm)—if existence were entirely absent, with the cessation of existence, would birth also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for birth, namely: existence.”

100. “It was said, ‘With `Upādānañca` (clinging) as condition, `bhavo` (existence) comes to be.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, with clinging as condition, existence comes to be. For if, Ānanda, clinging were not to exist in any way, in any manner, in any respect, for anyone, anywhere—namely, clinging to sensual pleasures, or clinging to views, or clinging to rules and observances, or clinging to a doctrine of self—then, in the total absence of clinging, with the cessation of clinging, would existence also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for existence, namely: clinging.”

101. “It was said, Ānanda: ‘With `taṇhā` (craving) as condition, there is `upādānaṃ` (clinging).’ This should be understood in this way: how it is that with craving as condition, there is clinging. For if, Ānanda, craving were entirely and in every way non-existent for anyone anywhere—namely, craving for forms, craving for sounds, craving for odors, craving for tastes, craving for tangibles, and craving for mental phenomena—then, with craving being completely absent and with the cessation of craving, would clinging also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for clinging, namely: craving.”

102. ‘With `vedanā` (feeling) as condition, `taṇhā` (craving) arises’—this, Ānanda, has been said; and in this way, by this method, it should be understood how with feeling as condition, craving arises. “If, Ānanda, feeling did not exist in any way, in any manner, in any respect, for anyone, anywhere—namely, feeling born of eye-contact, feeling born of ear-contact, feeling born of nose-contact, feeling born of tongue-contact, feeling born of body-contact, and feeling born of mind-contact—if feeling were entirely non-existent, with the cessation of feeling, would craving also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for craving; that is to say, feeling.”

103. Thus, Ānanda, in dependence on `vedanā` (feeling) there is `taṇhā` (craving); in dependence on craving there is seeking; in dependence on seeking there is gain; in dependence on gain there is decision-making; in dependence on decision-making there is desire and lust; in dependence on desire and lust there is attachment; in dependence on attachment there is possessiveness; in dependence on possessiveness there is stinginess; in dependence on stinginess there is guarding. Indeed, on account of guarding, many evil and unwholesome states arise: the taking up of sticks and weapons, quarrels, strife, disputes, recriminations, calumny, and falsehoods.

104. ‘On account of guarding, many evil unwholesome states arise: taking up sticks and weapons, quarrels, strife, disputes, recriminations, slander, and falsehoods’—this, Ānanda, has been said. And in this way, too, it should be understood how on account of guarding many evil unwholesome states arise: taking up sticks and weapons, quarrels, strife, disputes, recriminations, slander, and falsehoods. “Ānanda, if there were no protection at all, in any way, of anything for anyone anywhere—if, in the complete absence of protection, with the cessation of protection—would there arise those many evil, unwholesome states such as taking up sticks and weapons, quarrels, strife, disputes, mutual abuse, slander, and lying?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for the arising of many evil, unwholesome states—namely, taking up sticks and weapons, quarrels, strife, disputes, recriminations, slander, and falsehoods—which is to say: guarding.”

105. “It was said, ‘`macchariya` (Dependent upon stinginess) is guarding (*ārakkha*’). In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how guarding is dependent upon stinginess. For if, Ānanda, stinginess were not to exist in anyone, anywhere, in any way, at all—since stinginess is entirely absent and with the cessation of stinginess—would guarding also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for guarding, namely: stinginess.”

106. “It was said, ‘Stinginess is dependent on possessiveness.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how stinginess is dependent on possessiveness. For if, Ānanda, there were no possessiveness at all, in any way, of anything, for anyone, anywhere—if, in every respect, possessiveness were non-existent and through the cessation of possessiveness, would stinginess also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for stinginess, namely: possessiveness.”

107. “It was said: ‘Possession is dependent on attachment.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how possession is dependent on attachment. For if, Ānanda, there were no attachment at all, in any way, of anything, to anyone, anywhere—if, with the total absence of attachment and through the cessation of attachment, would possession also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for possession: namely, attachment.”

108. “It was said: ‘Dependent on desire and lust, there is fixation.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, dependent on desire and lust, there is fixation. For if, Ānanda, desire and lust were not to exist in anyone, anywhere, in any way, at all—when desire and lust are entirely absent, with the cessation of desire and lust, would fixation also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for fixation, namely: desire and lust.”

109. “It was said: ‘Dependent on discrimination, there is desire and lust.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, dependent on discrimination, there is desire and lust. For if, Ānanda, discrimination were entirely, in every way, and in all respects non-existent for anyone anywhere, then, with discrimination being completely absent and with the cessation of discrimination, would desire and lust also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for desire and lust, namely: discrimination.”

110. “It was said: ‘Discrimination is dependent on gain.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how discrimination is dependent on gain. For if, Ānanda, there were no gain at all, in any way, of anything, for anyone, anywhere—if gain were entirely non-existent and ceased—would discrimination also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for discrimination, namely: gain.”

111. “It was said, ‘Gain is dependent upon quest.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how gain is dependent upon quest. For if, Ānanda, there were no quest at all, in any way, of anything, for anyone, anywhere—since quest would be entirely absent and with the cessation of quest—would gain also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for gain, namely: quest.”

112. “‘Dependent upon `taṇhā` (craving) is seeking’—this, Ānanda, has been said; and in this way, too, it should be understood how, dependent upon craving, there is seeking. “If, Ānanda, craving were not to exist in any way, in any manner, in any respect, for anyone, or in anything—namely, craving for sensual pleasures, craving for existence, and craving for non-existence—if craving were entirely absent, with the cessation of craving, would any quest also be discerned?” “No, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for seeking: namely, craving.” Thus, Ānanda, these two things converge in a single meeting point within `vedanā` (feeling) by way of this twofold manner, indeed.”

113. “It has been said, ‘With `phasso` (contact) as condition, `vedanā` (feeling) arises.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, with contact as condition, feeling arises. For if, Ānanda, contact were entirely and in every way non-existent for anyone anywhere—namely, eye-contact, ear-contact, nose-contact, tongue-contact, body-contact, and mind-contact—if contact were completely absent, with the cessation of contact, would feeling also be discerned?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for feeling, namely: contact.”

114. “It has been said, ‘With name-and-form as condition, `phasso` (contact) arises.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, with name-and-form as condition, contact comes to be. “Ānanda, by whatever modes, whatever characteristics, whatever signs, and whatever indications there is the designation of the mental body, in the absence of those modes, those characteristics, those signs, and those indications, would designation-contact also be discerned in the material body?” “No, Bhante.” “Ānanda, by whatever aspects, whatever characteristics, whatever signs, and whatever indications the designation of the material body occurs—if those aspects... [as before] ...if those indications were absent, would contact through resistance be discernible in the mental body?” “No, Bhante.” “Ānanda, by whatever modes... [as before] ...by whatever summaries the designation of the mental body and the physical body occurs, if those modes... [as before] ...if those summaries were absent, would verbal contact or physical contact be discerned?” “No, Bhante.” “Ānanda, by whatever modes... [as before] ...by whatever summaries the designation of `nāmarūpa` (phenomenal objects) occurs, if those modes... [as before] ...if those summaries were absent, would contact be discerned?” “No, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for contact, namely: phenomenal objects.”

115. “It has been said, ‘With `Viññāṇañca` (consciousness) as condition, `nāmarūpaṃ` (phenomenal objects) arise.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, with consciousness as condition, phenomenal objects come to be. “Ānanda, if consciousness were not to descend into the mother’s womb, would phenomenal objects also coalesce in the mother’s womb?” “No, Bhante.” “Ānanda, if consciousness, having descended into the mother’s womb, were to depart, would phenomenal objects come to be produced for this present state of existence?” “No, Bhante.” “Ānanda, if consciousness were to be cut off while one is still a mere infant—a young boy or a young girl—would phenomenal objects then attain growth, increase, and expansion?” “No, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for phenomenal objects—namely, consciousness.”

116. “It has been said, ‘With name-and-form as condition, `viññāṇaṃ` (consciousness) arises.’ In this regard, Ānanda, it should be understood by this method how, with name-and-form as condition, consciousness comes to be. “Ānanda, if consciousness were not to find a footing in `nāmarūpa` (phenomenal objects), would there then be the manifestation of the arising of the mass of suffering—birth, aging, and death—in the future?” “No, indeed, Bhante.” “Therefore, Ānanda, this is the cause, this is the source, this is the `samudayo` (origin), this is the condition for consciousness, namely: phenomenal objects.” To this extent, Ānanda, one is born, or ages, or dies, or passes away, or reappears. To this extent is the path of designation, to this extent is the path of language, to this extent is the path of description, to this extent is the sphere of wisdom; to this extent does the round proceed for the purpose of describing this state, namely, that phenomenal objects together with consciousness rolls forward through mutual conditionality.”
